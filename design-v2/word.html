leverage

/ˈlev.ər.ɪdʒ/ verb
Practice this word 3 cards · about 2 minutes
In your language · 中文

利用；借助（已有资源）

What it means

To use something you already have — time, data, a relationship — to get a better result.

In workplace English, leverage usually means making the most of an existing resource. It is close to “use”, but it adds a sense of advantage: you are using what you have to get further.

In finance, leverage is also a noun meaning borrowed money. This page covers the verb you’ll hear in meetings and emails.

At work

Meeting

“If we leverage the research we already have, we can skip two weeks of discovery.”

Email

“Could we leverage the client’s existing dashboard instead of building a new one?”

Why this word matters at work

“Leverage” is a meeting word. It signals that you can see a resource — a dataset, a relationship, an existing process — and find a way to get more from it. Used well, it makes you sound resourceful. Used too often, it starts to sound like jargon, so keep it for moments when you really are making the most of something you already have.

If you are learning English for work, it is worth recognising even if you rarely say it. It shows up in strategy conversations, project reviews, and client emails — often in the phrase “leverage what we already have”.

Is leverage a verb or a noun?

Both. As a verb, it means to use something to get a better result: “We can leverage the data.” As a noun in finance, it means borrowed money or the amount of debt used to invest.

How is leverage different from “use”?

“Use” is neutral. “Leverage” adds the idea of advantage — you are using something you already have to get further, faster, or at lower cost.

Is leverage too formal for email?

It is common in professional email, especially in strategy, consulting, and tech. In a very friendly message, “use” may sound more natural.
